# S7 — Pricing

## Outcome

An owner opens **Precios** and sees, for every reference the network sells, what it costs, what it charges, what margin that leaves, and what price this stage proposes instead — computed from the reference's own measured price response where one exists, and from the tenant's own margin goal where it does not. Each proposal carries its arithmetic in the open: which of the two engines produced it, the current and projected margin, the estimated monthly impact in pesos, and — where the proposal rests on an elasticity — the coefficient itself, with the number of weeks and distinct price points it was fitted on. The owner approves lines, adjusts any number they disagree with, applies the set in one action, and — if the first week reads badly — reverts the whole batch in one more. Nothing this stage computes reaches a till except through a dated row in the price table, with a history, approved by a person.

**Two engines, because the screen has to be useful on the first morning** (§1, *Cold start*). The **margin rule** needs no history at all: a reference whose margin sits below the tenant's goal is proposed a price that moves toward the goal, bounded by the regulated cap and by the largest single step an owner permits. It works on day one, for every reference that has a cost and a price, which on a freshly loaded catalog is most of them. The **elasticity engine** appears per reference as that reference earns an estimate, and where it has one it decides. Every proposal states which engine produced it and how much demand signal stood behind it, because *this rests on a measured response* and *this rests on arithmetic about the margin you asked for* are two different claims, and an owner about to reprice a network is entitled to tell them apart at a glance.

The deck's claim is that most of a droguería's portfolio is price-inelastic: demand barely moves when the price does, so a small sustained adjustment on the right references lifts the month's margin without selling another unit. This stage is where that claim is either substantiated or exposed, and the screen is built to do both. **An item whose price has never moved has no elasticity, not a small one** — there is no variation to regress on, and waiting does not fix it, because what is missing is not observations but variation. That is not a limitation of the screen: **it is the reason the margin rule exists.** An elasticity does not degrade gracefully the way a demand forecast does (§1), so the estimator withholds rather than offering a weak number, records in one sentence which floor it missed, and hands the reference to the margin rule, which prices it on the owner's own goal and says so. The run's summary states how many references were evaluated, how many were estimable, how many were not and why, and how many proposals each engine produced. A pilot in which the estimable count collapses is a pilot in which the deck's claim is not yet substantiated — visibly, in the product, on the first Monday, which is the only useful time to find out — and the screen still had something to say that morning, which is the whole point.

The guardrail is the regulated maximum price. Colombian medicines under CNPMDM control carry a legal ceiling, and whether a maintained source of those ceilings exists is §11.4 and unanswered. This stage takes the only safe reading of a null cap: **unknown, never uncapped.** By default no reference whose cap status is unknown is ever proposed upward by **either** engine; it may be proposed downward, and it may be held. An owner may lift that default for the tenant, deliberately, with the act recorded. An item whose current shelf price already sits above a loaded cap is not a pricing opportunity at all — it is a compliance finding, and it is surfaced as one. **The margin rule sharpens this considerably.** An engine that mostly withholds could raise almost nothing on day one; an engine that needs only a cost and a goal can raise a great deal of the catalog on the first morning, with no history behind it. That makes an unknown ceiling materially more dangerous than it was, and it is why §11.4 is this stage's one real blocker while §11.2 is not (see *Gated on*).

The margin goal the whole product measures itself against lives here too, and after the cold-start decision it is no longer only a reference line: it is the margin rule's single input, so the number an owner types into `Ajustes` on the first morning is the number that prices the catalog that week. The Panel's `Margen bruto` tile draws **24,8%** against a **≥ 22%** reference (handoff, Pantalla 1); the `22` is a tenant setting in the `/api/settings/pricing` group this stage owns, S9 reads it rather than hard-coding it, and this stage prices against it.

## Scope

### In

1. **`elasticity_estimates`** — one row per item per grain per run, written for **every item evaluated**, including the ones that could not be estimated, with `elasticity` null and the reason recorded (§3, ledger).

9. **A stated answer for every reference that got no proposal.** An item is never silently absent: it is in the grid, with a badge distinguishing *nothing could evaluate this* from *both engines looked and there is nothing to do*, and a sentence naming which floor or which gate it missed.
10. **`items.custom` under the `pricing` key** — `elasticity_band`, `cap_status`, `last_model_price_at` (ledger, disputed columns).

**Why `elasticity.elasticity` must be nullable.** An item whose price has never moved does not have a small elasticity; it has no elasticity, because there is no variation to regress on. A zero in that column would be a claim of *perfect inelasticity* — the single most dangerous number this table can hold, because zero elasticity says "raise the price as far as you like and sell exactly as many". So the estimator writes a row with `elasticity = NULL`, `status = insufficient_variation`, and the counts that show why. Nothing downstream is allowed to coalesce that null to a number. If a build agent defaults it to `0`, every unmoved reference in the catalog — which is most of them — becomes a maximum-step price rise, and that is how this stage does damage.

**`item_prices.price` is the price the customer pays.** Architecture.md does not state it; this stage does, because margin is not computable without it. The shelf price is gross of IVA (handoff, `$15.600`), so **margin is computed net of IVA**: `net = price / (1 + rate(vat_class))`, `margin = (net − cost) / net`, with `excluded` and `exempt` at rate 0, `rate_5` at 0,05 and `rate_19` at 0,19 (§3). **Elasticity is estimated on the gross price**, because that is the number the customer saw. Two prices, two uses. *If this is wrong* — if S1 defines `price` as net — every margin figure in this stage, and therefore the Panel's `24,8%`, is overstated by up to 19% on the roughly one-third of a droguería's basket that is not IVA-excluded, and every `projected_margin` is unachievable. It is a one-line change here and a rebuild of S9's tile, so it is worth settling before either is built.

**The cost basis is current, never historical.** `cost_basis` is the quantity-weighted `lots.unit_cost` of the item's on-hand lots across the network at the moment the proposal is computed, falling back to `supplier_items.cost` where there is no stock and to null where there is neither — an item with no cost gets no proposal, because a margin computed against a missing cost is a fiction. `sale_lines.unit_cost` is deliberately **not** used: it is stamped per sale from the lot (ledger, disputed columns) and is exactly right for historical margin, and exactly wrong for projecting forward from today's replacement cost. The projection holds cost constant and the record panel says so — a proposal is a price decision, not a cost forecast.

### The estimator, and its limits

The columns above are meaningless without the method that fills them, so it is fixed here rather than left to a build agent. A pricing model does its damage through its assumptions, and an assumption nobody wrote down is an assumption nobody can review.

**Grain and window.** Weekly aggregates over the trailing **26 weeks**, per item, **pooled across sedes** with a location fixed effect where two or more sedes contributed. `window` records `26w`. The network grain (`location_id = NULL`) is the one a proposal reads, because a proposal is network-wide. A per-sede estimate is written only where that sede clears every floor on its own, and it is used to flag heterogeneity in the record panel — never to price a sede differently, which v1 cannot express.

**The estimator.** Ordinary least squares on `ln(units) = α + β·ln(price) + γ·t + month effects + location effects + ε`, with `β` reported as `elasticity`. The linear trend `t` absorbs slow drift; month effects are included only when the surviving weeks span three or more distinct months. `model_version` is `elasticity-loglog-v1`; changing the specification bumps it, and a batch never mixes versions.

**The observed price is what was paid**, `unit_price − discount` per unit from `sale_lines`, weighted by units — not the list price, because the customer responded to what they were charged.

**Exclusions, in the order they are applied.**

| Excluded | Why, and what it would do if it were not |
|---|---|
| Any week in which the item had zero sellable on-hand at a contributing location, replayed from `stock_moves` (§6) | **A stock-out reads as a demand collapse at an unchanged price.** That biases β *toward zero* — the item looks more inelastic than it is — and this engine raises the price of inelastic items. Left in, the model's confident recommendation is to raise the price of the product the sede could not supply. This is the most damaging failure mode in the stage and it is why S3's append-only ledger is a dependency rather than a nicety |
| Any week in which more than 30% of the item's units carried a discount | A promotion is a different demand regime, and mixing it in overstates responsiveness |
| Sales with `status = voided`, and returns | Not demand |
| Weeks with no `item_prices` row covering them | The price is unknown; an imputed price is an invented observation |
| Items with `active = false`, or `tracks_stock = false` where no cost exists | A service with no cost has no margin to project |

`weeks_excluded_stockout` and `weeks_excluded_promo` are stored and shown, so a reader can see how much of the window survived.

**Floors. An estimate is written at all only when** at least **8** weeks survive, **and** the window contains at least **2 distinct prices** with at least **3 surviving weeks at each of the two most-used**, **and** the standard deviation of `ln(price)` is at least **0,02**. Below any of these, the row is written with a null `elasticity` and the matching `status`. Most of a droguería's catalog lands here on day one, the screen says so rather than hiding it, and the margin rule is what keeps that screen worth opening while it is true.

**An elasticity proposal is offered only when**, in addition: `observations ≥ 12`, `r2 ≥ 0,30`, `confidence` is `medium` or better, the reference has not been priced by the model within `min_days_between_changes`, and the projected monthly impact clears the materiality floor. `confidence` is **`high`** at `observations ≥ 20`, `r2 ≥ 0,50` and a 90% interval on β no wider than 0,6; **`medium`** at the offer floor; **`low`** otherwise — and a `low` estimate never becomes an **elasticity** proposal, so an elasticity-backed proposal is only ever `high` or `medium`. **A `margin_rule` proposal carries `confidence = low` by construction**, and that is not a demerit in it: its arithmetic is exact, and what is unknown is how demand will respond, which is precisely what `confidence` measures on this table. An owner who filters the grid to `Confianza · Alta` is asking to see only what was measured, and gets exactly that.

**The engine does not solve for an optimum, and that is deliberate.** Under constant elasticity the margin-maximising price is `c·β/(1+β)`, which for an inelastic item (`|β| < 1`) is negative or unbounded — the formula's answer for most of this catalog is "infinity", which is obviously false and is the trap this method sets. So the engine evaluates a **bounded step** instead: candidate prices on a grid from `−max_single_step_pct` to `+max_single_step_pct`, each rounded to `rounding_unit`, each scored as `Δprofit ≈ q̂₃₀ · [ (net(p₁) − c)·(p₁/p₀)^β − (net(p₀) − c) ]`, and the highest-scoring candidate wins. `q̂₃₀` is the item's trailing 30-day units across the network after the same exclusions. **For an inelastic item the winner is always the largest permitted step**, which means the step cap *is* the pricing policy and it belongs to a human — which is why `max_single_step_pct` is a setting an owner sets and not a number the model chooses. *If this bounded-step decision is wrong*, the model leaves money on an inelastic reference where a larger move would have earned more; the remedy is an owner raising the cap after a batch has been measured, not a model extrapolating a local fit to a price nobody has ever charged.

**Rounding is toward the current price, never away from it.** A candidate is rounded to the nearest `rounding_unit` that does not carry the step past `max_single_step_pct` — so a 3% cap on `$12.500` at a `$50` unit yields `$12.850` (+2,8%), not `$12.900` (+3,2%). A rounding rule that can exceed a stated policy limit is a rounding rule that quietly rewrites the policy.

**The arithmetic, worked once, because a build agent should not have to infer it.** Acetaminofén 500 mg × 100, Genfar. `vat_class = excluded`, so gross and net are the same figure. Cost basis `$8.400` from the weighted `lots.unit_cost`; current price `$12.500`; trailing 30-day units across the network `1.180`; the fit returns `β = −0,34`, `r2 = 0,41`, `observations = 22` — `high` confidence. With `max_single_step_pct = 3,0` and `rounding_unit = 50`, the winning candidate is `$12.850`, a step of +2,8%.

| | Now | Proposed |
|---|---|---|
| Price | `$12.500` | `$12.850` |
| Margin, net of IVA | `(12.500 − 8.400) / 12.500` = **32,8%** | `(12.850 − 8.400) / 12.850` = **34,6%** |
| Monthly units | `1.180` | `1.180 × (12.850/12.500)^−0,34` ≈ **1.169** |
| Monthly contribution | `1.180 × 4.100` = `$4.838.000` | `1.169 × 4.450` = `$5.202.050` |

`estimated_monthly_impact` = **`+$364.050`**, `current_margin` = 32,8%, `projected_margin` = 34,6%, `step_pct` = 2,8, `reason_code` = `inelastic_raise`. Eleven fewer units a month, and the margin moves **+1,8 pp** — which is the deck's claim, in one reference, with every input on screen. It is also the shape of the Panel's `+1,9 pp desde el ajuste de precios` footnote, and the reason S9 can compute that footnote at all.

**Materiality.** An elasticity proposal is offered only when `|estimated_monthly_impact| ≥ $50.000` and the price moves by at least one `rounding_unit`. This is a constant in the engine and **deliberately not a setting**, because a setting that controls how many proposals appear is a dial someone will turn until the screen looks productive. A screen of 1.200 rows each worth six hundred pesos is a screen nobody reviews, and an unreviewed batch is the failure this whole surface is shaped to prevent.

**The margin rule's materiality is a margin gap, not a peso impact**, and the reason is structural rather than stylistic: on day one there is no trailing volume, so there is no peso figure to compare against a floor, and a rule that demanded one would fire on nothing precisely when the screen most needs to fire. A margin-rule proposal is offered when the reference sits at least **1,0 pp** below `margin_goal_pct` **and** the price moves by at least one `rounding_unit`. Where trailing volume exists the peso impact is computed at unchanged volume and shown — labelled as the assumption it is — and it orders the grid; it never gates it.

**Seasonality is partly handled and says so.** A week-of-year term is not estimable from 26 weeks and this document does not pretend otherwise: the fit carries a trend and month effects, and models no annual cycle. The bias it leaves points at *withholding*: a price rise that coincided with a seasonal fall reads as elastic, which suppresses a raise rather than producing a bad one. The residual risk is the mirror case — an elastic reading that produces a **decrease** on a seasonal item — and it is bounded by the same step cap, the same materiality floor and a human pressing a button on every line. A second year of history is what actually fixes this, and nothing in the schema needs to change when it arrives.

**Competitor effects are not handled at all, because there is no source.** β is a reduced-form own-price response that already contains whatever competitors did in the same weeks. For a price the pharmacy controls that is the right object to estimate. Where it fails is a market-wide move everybody followed, which is attributed to us; the guard is that `computed_at`, `window` and `model_version` are on every estimate, so a batch made in a month the whole market moved is identifiable afterwards rather than untraceable.

**Imported history is used and labelled.** Where S6's loader has run, `sales.source = imported` rows enter the fit — they carry a quantity and a price, which is all β needs — and `imported_share` records what fraction of the window they are. A proposal computed on a majority-imported window is capped at `confidence = medium`, because those rows record a price the previous system charged and a cost we did not pay. Where S6 has not run, the window is short, `observations` is low, few references clear the floors, and the screen says exactly that (§11.2).

**The regulated cap, as a rule, and it binds both engines.** `regulated_max_price` null means **unknown**, never uncapped (ledger, disputed columns). The three cap states live in `items.custom.pricing.cap_status`: `capped` (a cap is loaded), `not_regulated` (a source states the item is not under control), `unknown` (nobody has said). Every item starts `unknown`. Nothing below distinguishes a proposal by its basis, because a legal ceiling does not care which arithmetic reached it. Then:

- `respects_regulated_cap` is `true` only when a cap is known **and** the proposed price is at or below it. **An unknown cap yields `false`**, because a boolean named *respects the regulated cap* cannot be true against a cap nobody holds.
- An item at `unknown` may be proposed **downward** or held, and may be proposed **upward only** when `allow_raise_without_cap` is on. It is **off** by default, so on a tenant where nobody has loaded a cap **the margin rule proposes nothing upward either** — which is the day-one behaviour §11.4 actually governs. Turning it on is an ordinary settings write behind the ordinary permission dependency (ledger), and it writes an `audit_log` row and puts a standing line on the screen while it is on.
- An item at `capped` whose optimal candidate exceeds the cap is proposed **at the cap**, and its reason states that the cap bound it.
- An item whose **current** price already exceeds a loaded cap is not a pricing opportunity. It is written with `status = above_cap`, `respects_regulated_cap = false`, and it is a compliance finding: the till is charging above the legal maximum today. It renders `Sobre el tope regulado` in critical and **no role can approve it** (§B.8.4·1) — which incidentally settles design-system.md §B.17·3's open question about whether an `admin` may approve above a cap: nobody may.

*If §11.4 comes back "no maintained source exists"*, every item stays `unknown`, and with the default off **this stage offers no upward proposal at all** and reduces to a downward-and-diagnostics tool. That is the intended failure mode and it is not a defect. The escape that makes the stage useful anyway is the manual cap path: a regente can enter caps for the few dozen references they actually sell under control, or load them as a CSV, and those references become priceable immediately.

### The margin rule, and which engine wins

The estimator measures what a reference's demand does. The margin rule measures nothing: it applies the tenant's own margin goal to the tenant's own cost, which is exactly why it works on a catalog loaded yesterday, and exactly why it is labelled as an assumption everywhere it appears (§1, *parametric*).

**What it computes.** For an item that is `active`, carries a cost basis and has an in-force price, the target gross price is `p* = c · (1 + rate(vat_class)) / (1 − margin_goal_pct)` — the price at which the net-of-IVA margin equals the goal. The proposal is `min(p*, p₀ · (1 + max_single_step_pct), regulated_max_price)`, rounded to `rounding_unit` toward the current price by the same rule the estimator uses, so the stated step cap is never exceeded by a rounding. **The margin rule only ever proposes upward.** A reference already at or above the goal is not a defect to correct, and lowering a price in order to *reduce* a margin is not a thing this product does.

**It reaches the goal in steps, not in one move**, and the reason line says so. A reference at 15,2% against a 22,0% goal needs an 8,7% rise and `max_single_step_pct` is 3,0, so the proposal is the capped step, `margin_gap_pp` records what remains, and the reference is proposed again after `min_days_between_changes`. *If this is wrong*, an owner waits three months for a reference to reach goal and the remedy is theirs — raise the step cap, having watched what the first step did to volume. *If the opposite were built*, one 8,7% jump lands on a reference nobody has measured, which is the move that sends a customer to the droguería across the street, and no engine here has the evidence to justify it.

**Worked once, so nobody has to infer it.** Loratadina 10 mg × 10, `vat_class = excluded`, cost basis `$3.900`, current price `$4.600`, goal 22,0%, step cap 3,0%, `rounding_unit` 50. Current margin `(4.600 − 3.900) / 4.600` = **15,2%**. The goal price is `3.900 / 0,78` = `$5.000`, a +8,7% move, past the cap — so the candidate is `4.600 × 1,03` = `$4.738`, rounded toward the current price to **`$4.700`** (+2,2%). `projected_margin` **17,0%**, `margin_gap_pp` **5,0**, `basis = margin_rule`, `confidence = low`, `reason_code = margin_below_goal`. With trailing 30-day volume of 220 units the impact shown is `220 × 100` = **`+$22.000` al mes, si el volumen no cambia**; with no trailing sales at all `estimated_monthly_impact` is **null**, the cell reads `Sin volumen`, and the proposal stands anyway — the margin arithmetic never needed the volume.

**No category defaults, deliberately.** §1 permits a parametric model to use category-level defaults stated as assumptions; this stage uses none. A default margin per therapeutic class would be a claim about what a category ought to earn, made by us, about a market we do not sell in — and it would arrive on the screen wearing the same badge as the owner's own goal. The only parametric inputs here are the tenant's: `margin_goal_pct`, `max_single_step_pct`, `rounding_unit`, and whatever caps a regente has loaded. *If this is wrong*, the screen proposes one goal for a category that could never bear it, the owner rejects those lines, and the rejections are the signal that a per-category goal is worth building — one settings key and no schema change.

**Which engine owns a reference.** The partial unique constraint above permits one live proposal per reference, so precedence is decided here rather than blended:

| The reference's estimate | Who prices it |
|---|---|
| Qualifies — `confidence` `medium` or `high` | **The elasticity engine, whatever it concludes**, including concluding that nothing is worth doing. A measured response beats an assumption in both directions, and a reference the estimator priced or deliberately left alone is not re-proposed upward by the margin rule because a goal says it should earn more |
| Exists, does not qualify, and reads **elastic** (point `β ≤ −1`) | **Neither engine raises it.** `no_proposal_reason = elastic_veto`. The estimate is too weak to price on and strong enough to veto: weak evidence may stop a move and may not start one |
| Exists, does not qualify, and reads inelastic — or `elasticity` is null | **The margin rule**, on the terms above |
| No estimate at all — day one, a new reference, no sales in the window | **The margin rule** |

*If this precedence is wrong* — if a measured response should defer to the margin goal on a reference sitting far below it — the reference is still in the grid with its estimate and its margin both in the record panel, and the owner may override the price by hand and apply it. The reverse error is not recoverable in the same way: a margin rule permitted to override a measured elastic response would raise the price of exactly the references whose customers leave, under a badge saying the model recommended it.

**The gates bind both engines.** The cap rules above, `min_days_between_changes`, the one-live-proposal constraint, the batch cap and the apply-time re-validation apply to a proposal whatever produced it; none of them is an elasticity concern. A `margin_rule` proposal is an ordinary row in `price_proposals` and is approved, applied and reverted by the same paths, which is what keeps the second engine a second **input** rather than a second product.

**Apply re-validates, because a proposal is a week old by the time it is applied.** A batch computed Monday and applied Friday rests on a cost and a price that may both have moved, and `item_prices` is a table two stages write. So `POST /api/pricing/apply`, inside its transaction and before writing anything, re-checks every proposal in the set and **refuses the whole batch** if any fails, naming the failures in the response:

- **The in-force price no longer equals `current_price`.** Someone — S1's catalog surface, or an earlier batch — changed this price after the proposal was computed. Applying anyway would silently overwrite a deliberate human decision with a stale model one. This is the collision that follows from `item_prices` having two writers (ledger), and it is the reason this check exists rather than a defensive habit.
- **The cost basis has moved more than 5%** since `computed_at`. The proposal's whole case is a projected margin, and a margin projected against a cost that has since moved is not the number the owner approved.
- **A cap now binds** that did not when the proposal was made, or `cap_status` moved to `unknown` while `allow_raise_without_cap` is off.

The response lists which references failed and why, the screen re-renders them with their new figures, and the owner re-runs or applies the rest. Refusing the whole batch rather than the failing lines is deliberate: an owner who approved twelve references and got eleven has an inventory of what happened that nobody will reconcile.

**Who may approve, and who may apply.** §2 grants `owner` "price approval" and defines `admin` as `owner` minus hard delete, role changes and billing/API-key settings — so by subtraction an `admin` may approve a price, and design-system.md §B.17·3 records the per-action detail as still open. This stage splits it: **an `admin` may approve, reject and override individual proposals; only an `owner` may apply or revert a batch, or trigger a run.** The reason is blast radius — a bulk price change across a network is the largest-consequence action in the product and nothing else in §2 resembles it. *If this is wrong*, Marcela Ríos assembles a batch and has to ask the owner to press one button, which is a friction worth one line of code to remove. *If the opposite is wrong*, a network-wide price change happens that nobody senior authorised, which is not.

**Settings group** (ledger rule 5; the first three keys are the register's, the last two are added inside the group S7 owns):

| Key | Default | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| `margin_goal_pct` | **unset** | The Panel's `≥ 22%` reference, the `Margen proyectado` tile's target marker, and **the margin rule's only input**. **Deliberately shipped with no default**, for the reason this stage gives for rejecting category margin defaults: a number we chose arrives on the screen wearing the same badge as the owner's own goal, and `22,0` looks like a finding about their business rather than a placeholder we picked. Until an owner sets it the margin rule proposes nothing and says why, and S9 renders `Sin meta definida` — a reachable state rather than dead code. **Setting it is an onboarding step** (S10's provisioning), which takes an owner about ten seconds and is the moment they decide what the number should be. `22,0` is the handoff's illustration and the demo seed's value, never a shipped default. **S9 reads this key** |
| `max_single_step_pct` | `3,0` | The largest move a proposal from either engine may make. For an inelastic reference, and for every margin-rule reference, this *is* the pricing policy |
| `allow_raise_without_cap` | `false` | Whether an item at `cap_status = unknown` may be proposed upward, by either engine |
| `min_days_between_changes` | `30` | A reference the model priced within this window is not re-proposed. What makes an adjustment sustained rather than a weekly ratchet — without it, 3% a week compounds to 42% a year |
| `rounding_unit` | `50` | Proposals round to this many pesos. `$15.637` is not a price a droguería charges |

## Jobs

Both jobs take `tenant_id` in the payload and pin inside their own transaction before touching anything (ledger rule 6). Both hold a Procrastinate lock keyed `pricing:{tenant_id}` (§9), so a run and a cap check never interleave, and `POST /api/pricing/apply` is refused with a named message while either holds it.

**`pricing.run`** — the estimator, the margin rule and the proposal engine as **one job**, not three.

- **Trigger:** cron, weekly, Monday 03:00 in the tenant's timezone (`/api/settings/tenant`, S0). Also on demand from `POST /api/pricing/runs`.
- **Idempotency key:** `(tenant_id, 'pricing.run', window_end_date, model_version)`. A second enqueue for the same window and version is a no-op, so a retried cron and an owner pressing `Calcular ahora` on the same morning produce one run.
- **Shape:** aggregation and regression happen in memory, the estimator runs first and the proposal engine then consults its output to decide which engine owns each reference; **the entire run's estimates and proposals are written in one transaction at the end.** A long write transaction holding four thousand regressions open is the thing not to build, and a half-written run is the thing a screen must never read.
- **The run never no-ops for want of sales.** With zero `sales` rows the estimator writes a `no_sales` row for every evaluated item and the margin rule still produces proposals from cost and price alone. The day-one path is the ordinary path with one engine silent — not a special case, not a separate command — and it is the path the demo seed exercises.
- **Superseding:** proposals still `proposed` or `above_cap` from an earlier run move to `superseded` in the same transaction. A proposal at `approved` that was never applied is superseded only once it is **14 days** old, and the run's summary states how many. A proposal at `applied` is never touched.
- **Failure:** three attempts with backoff. All three failing leaves the previous run's estimates and proposals exactly as they were — nothing is partially replaced — and the screen's freshness line goes stale and shows its age. The screen additionally renders a region-scope error naming the run and its date (§B.10.3). It does not blank the table: last week's proposals are still the best information that exists.

**`pricing.check_caps`** — the guardrail, run independently of the model.

- **Trigger:** cron, daily, 05:00 tenant time.
- **What it does:** compares every item's in-force price against its `regulated_max_price` and raises `status = above_cap` for any item now charging above a loaded cap. This exists because a cap can move without us: a CNPMDM circular lowers a ceiling and a price that was legal on Tuesday is not on Wednesday. Tying that check to a weekly model run would leave a pharmacy up to six days over the line.
- **Idempotency key:** `(tenant_id, 'pricing.check_caps', date)`. Re-running produces the same rows.
- **Failure:** retried; a persistent failure surfaces on the screen as the `Propuestas sobre el tope` tile carrying its own staleness reading (§B.9.2), because a compliance tile that silently stops updating is worse than one that is absent.

**Application and revert are deliberately not jobs.** They are synchronous, transactional endpoints. A batch of a few hundred rows is nothing for Postgres, and a half-applied price change is precisely the outcome that makes a droguería distrust the feature forever. All rows or none, and the owner sees the result before the dialog closes. **A batch is capped at 500 references** and above that the request is refused with a message telling the owner to narrow the filter — see *Acceptance* for why that cap is a counter concern and not an ergonomic one.

## Offline

**Precios is an online-only office surface, and it says so plainly.** It is `owner`/`admin`, seated, server-authoritative, server-paginated, and it is **not** in S2's sync registry — this stage amends that registry in no way (A4, ledger rule 9). With no network the route renders the route-scope error geometry with a retry and a correlation id (§B.10.3). It does **not** render a cached table: a stale price proposal is a number someone would approve, and a proposal approved against last week's cost, last week's cap and last week's stock is exactly the mistake the whole guardrail exists to prevent.

**What this stage sends to a till, it sends through a path that already exists.** Applying a batch writes `item_prices` rows; those rows reach every device on its next delta pull, over S2's existing replication (A4). There is no new sync path, no push from this stage, and nothing for a cashier to do. A future-dated row is safe to carry early, because the price in force is resolved from `effective_from`/`effective_to` at read time rather than from a state someone flips.

**A till that is offline through a price change keeps charging the old price, and that is correct.** §5's stale-price reconciliation is already decided: the sale stands at the price actually charged, `sale_lines.unit_price` is the record, and the difference is reported rather than corrected. **This stage is the main producer of that condition** — before S7 exists, prices change rarely and by hand; after it, they change in batches on a schedule. Two consequences follow and both are stated rather than discovered. First, `effective_from` is a **floor, not a guarantee**: a sede offline all afternoon charges the old price until it syncs, which is a second reason the default effective date is tomorrow 00:00 rather than now. Second, the reconciliation report §5 promises will have real volume for the first time, and S9's margin figures must read `sale_lines.unit_price` rather than the price list, or a week with an offline sede will not reconcile.

**The batch cap is a counter concern.** §4 budgets a delta pull with no changes at **< 20ms p95**, and an applied batch is the largest single burst of changed rows this product ever produces for a device. 500 references is the cap, and it is chosen so a batch is one ordinary delta rather than a pull a till waits on with a customer in front of it. An owner who wants to reprice four thousand references does it as eight batches, each of which is separately revertible — which is better practice anyway. **The margin rule makes this cap bind sooner than the estimator ever would.** On a freshly loaded catalog it can find several hundred references below goal at once, where the elasticity engine on the same catalog finds none, so the first batch after go-live is the likeliest in the product's life to hit 500. That is the cap working, not a defect, and it is said here so nobody reads the refusal as one on the first day it appears.

**The settings section is online-only for the same reason and with a sharper edge.** `Precios y topes` lives in the settings dialog, which floats over whatever route is showing (§B.8.4·4); with no network it renders the section's field stack in the region-scope error treatment rather than a form whose `Guardar` will fail. A settings form that accepts input it cannot persist is worse than one that refuses, because the owner walks away believing the margin goal changed.

**Staleness on this surface** is a single per-surface line, not forty dots: the provenance line in the filter bar states the run's `computed_at`, which is §B.9.2's one permitted exception for a surface whose entire content is one snapshot. Every other figure on the screen is server-live and carries no marker.

## Demo seed

S1 owns the command; this stage registers its own fixtures with it, and **S7 is not finished until Precios renders convincingly from the seed** (§1, ledger *Cross-stage services*) — a sharper test than the suite, because it is what catches the tile whose denominator is zero and the empty state nobody designed. Everything below is synthetic and self-evidently so, and none of it is ever loaded into a real tenant.

- **The settings group at its defaults** — `margin_goal_pct` 22,0, `max_single_step_pct` 3,0, `rounding_unit` 50, `min_days_between_changes` 30, `allow_raise_without_cap` **false**. The seed never demonstrates the product with the guardrail switched off.
- **Caps in all three states, in a realistic proportion.** Roughly 60 references at `cap_status = capped` with a `regulated_max_price` and a plainly fictional source reference, a few hundred at `not_regulated`, the rest at `unknown` — the state a real tenant reaches after a regente spends half an hour on the references they actually sell under control. **Two capped references are seeded above their cap**, so `Propuestas sobre el tope` is non-zero, the critical badge renders and the absent-checkbox rule is visible without anyone constructing it by hand.
- **Margin-rule proposals, the bulk of the screen** — around 180 references below the margin goal, computed from the seeded catalog's own costs and prices, **none of them at `cap_status = unknown`**, because the default forbids raising those and a seed that contradicted its own engine would teach a build agent the wrong rule. A handful are cap-bound so `cap_bound_raise` renders, and a handful have no trailing sales so the `Sin volumen` impact cell renders.
- **Elasticity proposals, the minority** — around 30 references whose synthetic sales carry two or three price points across the 26-week window, producing real fits rather than stamped numbers: mostly `inelastic_raise` across `high` and `medium` confidence, two `elastic_reduce`, and at least one `elastic_veto` where a weak elastic reading blocks a margin raise. This is the mix the screen exists to show, and it is what puts something on both sides of the `Base` chip.
- **Every no-proposal state populated at least once** — each `elasticity_status` and each `no_proposal_reason` value on some reference, because `Sin evaluar` and `Sin propuesta` can only be checked for conflation side by side.
- **One applied batch, dated about ten days back**, with its `item_prices` rows written and the superseded rows closed — so `Lotes aplicados` is not empty, the revert path is exercisable in a demo, a handful of references sit in `cooldown` with the reason rendered, and S9 has a real batch to compute its `+1,9 pp desde el ajuste de precios` footnote against.

**The seed runs this stage's own engines rather than inserting proposal rows by hand.** It seeds prices, costs, caps and sales, then invokes `pricing.run`. A fixture that bypasses the engine is a fixture that keeps rendering a convincing screen after the engine has broken, which is the one thing a completion test must not do.

## Hands off

- **`elasticity_estimates`**, one row per evaluated item per run, with a **nullable** `elasticity` and a `status` that says why it is null. Anything reading this table treats null as *no estimate* and never coalesces it to zero.
- **`price_proposals`**, with the full `price_proposal_status` enum, `approved_price` alongside `proposed_price`, and `batch_id` grouping every applied set. **This is the table S9's `+1,9 pp desde el ajuste de precios` footnote is computed from**: the batches, their dates and their estimated impact are the record that makes the claim measurable against `daily_metrics`.
- **`item_prices` rows at `source = model`**, dated, with the row they superseded closed — and the caveat a reader needs: a revert also writes `source = model`, because `source` names who wrote the row. **The honest measure of "prices the model set and that stuck" is `price_proposals.status`, not a `source` filter on the price table.** S9 must read the proposal table for that number.
- **`item_prices.regulated_max_price`** populated for whatever caps exist, and `items.custom.pricing.cap_status` distinguishing `capped`, `not_regulated` and `unknown` — so no later stage has to re-derive "we have no cap" from a null.
- **The `/api/settings/pricing` group**, with `margin_goal_pct` as the key S9's Panel tile and the Reportes margin reports read. It is a settings read, not a constant, and not a copy.
- **The Precios screen**, built to §B.8.4·1, and the `Precios y topes` settings section in the dialog's **Operación** group — the second and third of the seven undrawn surfaces to land.
- **No change to S2's sync registry, no new sync path, and no new job-facing contract.** A price change is an ordinary row in an already-synced table.
- **The reason-code vocabulary**, fixed and closed. S9's margin reports render the same strings for the same codes rather than composing their own explanation of why a reference was or was not repriced, and no surface in the product explains a price with generated text.
- **The apply-time re-validation contract** — a proposal is checked against the in-force price, the current cost and the current cap before it is written, and a batch is all-or-nothing. Any later stage that writes `item_prices` inherits the obligation not to change a price out from under an approved batch without that batch failing loudly.
- **The batch cap of 500 references**, which every later stage that writes many rows to a synced table should treat as the precedent: a bulk office action's real budget is the delta pull it produces on a till (§4).
- **`price_proposals.basis`** — the §1 basis for this stage's rows, `margin_rule` or `elasticity`, on every proposal, alongside the `confidence` that says how much demand signal stood behind it. **S9 must not aggregate the two into one claim.** A margin-rule proposal's `estimated_monthly_impact` is computed at unchanged volume and is an assumption; an elasticity proposal's is a projection from a fitted coefficient. Applied batches are the record either way, and the Panel's `+1,9 pp desde el ajuste de precios` footnote is measured against `daily_metrics` rather than against either estimate — but any report that presents estimated impact as a forecast must state what share of it was assumed.
- **The seed fixtures**, registered with S1's demo-seed command: caps in all three states, proposals from both engines including one above-cap compliance finding, every no-proposal reason populated once, and one applied batch — which is also the fixture S9 computes its price-adjustment footnote against.

## Gated on

- **§11.4 — regulated maximum prices. This is the stage's one blocker, and the cold-start decision made it more important rather than less** (§1). Before this stage had a margin rule, an unanswered §11.4 was survivable: the elasticity engine withheld on most of the catalog anyway, so a missing ceiling blocked proposals that mostly did not exist. **The margin rule raises prices on day one, from a cost and a goal, with no history behind it** — which is what makes the screen useful and, in the same stroke, what makes an unknown legal ceiling materially more dangerous. The stage ships either way and states its own safe position: a null cap is *unknown*, an unknown cap blocks an upward proposal from **either** engine unless an owner permits it tenant-wide and on the record, and a price already above a loaded cap is a compliance finding rather than a pricing opportunity. **What is blocked is the demo and the pilot's first week**: with no cap source and the default in force, both engines reduce to downward proposals and diagnostics, which is not what the deck sells. Three answers unblock it at very different prices — a maintained source of CNPMDM caps; a decision that the pilot sells nothing under control, which costs nothing and is worth asking in exactly that form; or a regente loading caps for the few dozen controlled references the network actually stocks, which is half an hour of work and is what the demo seed models.
- **§11.2 — what the legacy system can export, above all sales history. Not a blocker** (§1). It was written as one and it is not. With no import at all the margin rule prices the catalog on day one, the demo seed fills every tile, and the pilot is demonstrable and runnable. What the export changes is *which engine is doing the work*: with it, elasticity appears immediately on the references that moved price inside the imported window and the screen leads with measured responses; without it, the 26-week window starts filling on go-live day, the first genuinely useful fits arrive roughly six months later, and until then most rows read `Margen` rather than `Elasticidad`. That is a quality and timing variable. The honest question at a pilot review is *how much of this screen is evidence yet*, the `Base` chip answers it in one click, and the answer being *not much, on the first Monday* is the expected reading rather than a defect. Worth chasing early because it is free accuracy; not worth blocking a pilot for.
